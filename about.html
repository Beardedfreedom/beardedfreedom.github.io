whoami

beardedfreedom

Security researcher. USAF combat veteran. The person who reads the binary so you don't have to.

I spent my service learning that the boring details are the ones that get people hurt — the undocumented setting, the assumption nobody wrote down, the gap between what a system says it does and what it actually does. That instinct never turned off. It just changed targets.

These days I hunt bugs on HackerOne and pull apart tools I depend on — and I depend on Claude Code. It's in my pipeline, my terminal, my muscle memory. So when a new build drops, I do what I'd do with any black box in a position of trust: I read it. The flags, the strings, the native modules, the things that changed between this version and the last.

Most of what I find isn't a vulnerability. It's just undocumented — a flag that flips behavior, a model that quietly rotated out, a hook that now fires earlier. Useful to know, published nowhere. This site is where I write it down, version by version, in plain language. The changelog Anthropic doesn't publish — not because they're hiding it, but because nobody ships patch notes at the depth a power user actually needs.

the ethos

Green lane, red lane

One rule decides what goes on this site and what never does. It's simple enough to state in a sentence, and I hold it without exception.

Publish how it works

How the tool behaves. What a flag does. What changed between builds. Architecture, intent, the why behind a design. Knowledge that makes you a sharper, safer operator. This is the whole site. If it helps you understand the thing you already trust, it ships.

Never publish how to break it

Working exploits. Weaponized payloads. Evasions, bypasses, anything whose primary use is harm. If a finding crosses into that lane it goes through coordinated disclosure — quietly, to the vendor — and it stays off this site until it's fixed, and even then only the how-it-works half is told.

Understanding is a public good. Exploitation is not. — the line I don't cross

That's the combat-engineer ethos applied to software: know the terrain better than anyone, so you can protect the people moving across it. I'm a guest in Anthropic's codebase, not an adversary. This site is unaffiliated with Anthropic — just a researcher who respects the tool enough to read it closely and report the rest the right way.
